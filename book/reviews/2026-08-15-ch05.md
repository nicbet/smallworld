## Issues by Category

### 1. General-before-Smallworld violations (most pervasive issue)

The biggest pattern problem: several sections lead with Smallworld's specific types/code before establishing the general principle. Chapter 04 does this well (e.g., three clocks are explained generally before the `Time` struct appears). Chapter 05 often inverts this.

**Sections that need reordering:**

- **"winit as the Platform Adapter" (line 49):** Jumps straight into Smallworld's specific crate choice. Should first discuss what a platform adapter does in general, how other engines handle this boundary (Unreal's FGenericPlatformMisc/FPlatformApplicationMisc, Unity's native player loop, Godot's DisplayServer), then present `winit` as Smallworld's answer.

- **"WindowState" (line 62):** Goes directly to Smallworld's struct. Should first establish the general principle that platform metadata (window dimensions, focus, scale factor) is categorically different from device signals, discuss how other engines separate these (Unreal's `FGenericWindow`, Unity's `Screen` class), then show `WindowState`.

- **"The Raw Polling API" (line 88):** Drops the `Input` struct listing immediately. Should first discuss the general concept of raw device polling in engines, how other engines expose this layer (Unity's `Keyboard.current[Key.W]`, Godot's `Input.is_key_pressed()`), then present Smallworld's design.

- **"ControllerState" (line 141):** The struct appears without any general discussion of how engines model controller state.

- **"Smallworld's Platform Boundary" (line 421):** Even the *heading* is Smallworld-specific. Should be "The Platform Boundary" with the general principle first, Smallworld as illustration.


### 2. Missing industry stories and historical grounding

The Action Mapping Layer section (line 165) and the Fixed-Tick Edge Problem (line 315) are excellent models of historical grounding. But many other sections are pure mechanical description with no industry context:

- **Edge Detection (line 113):** No industry context at all. This is a great place for a story about bugs caused by missing edge detection, or the evolution from event-driven to polled-with-edges.

- **Analog Inputs (line 129):** No mention of high-polling-rate mice in competitive FPS (1000+ Hz mice generating dozens of events per frame), controller drift issues that plagued Nintendo Switch Joy-Cons, or how competitive games like CS:GO treat raw mouse input.

- **Context Stack (line 233):** No comparison with how other engines solve this. UE5's Enhanced Input uses InputMappingContexts with explicit priorities. Unity's Input System uses action maps that can be enabled/disabled. Godot uses `_unhandled_input` propagation. These are all variations on the same idea, and the comparison would strengthen the section.

- **Rebinding and Persistence (line 258):** No accessibility context. The CVAA (21st Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act) requires remapping in games with communication features. Microsoft's Xbox Accessibility Guidelines and Sony's accessibility requirements make remapping a certification prerequisite. TGA now has a Best Accessibility category. This is rich industry material that is entirely missing.

- **Mouse Capture (line 358):** No competitive gaming stories. Raw input vs. cursor warp is a well-known topic in competitive FPS, and Windows historically had issues with mouse acceleration and raw input that `winit` had to work around.

- **Replay and Determinism (line 366):** Could mention fighting games (GGPO rollback netcode relies on input recording), RTS games (StarCraft's replay system records only inputs), and racing games (ghost replay). These are well-known industry examples that would ground the section.


### 3. Heading discipline violation

**"#### Composite Bindings and Dead Zones" (line 221)** is the only `####` under "### The Action Mapping Layer". This is a lone subsection, which violates the rule: "A heading earns its place only when it divides its parent into two or more peer ideas." Either add a sibling heading (e.g., split into "#### Composite Bindings" and "#### Dead Zones") or fold this content into the parent section as a bold lead-in paragraph.


### 4. Thin sections that need more substance

Per the guideline "meaty, verbose paragraphs":

- **"Rebinding and Persistence" (line 258-266):** Only ~200 words of body text before the callout. This should be one of the meatier sections given how important rebinding is to shipped games. Needs accessibility context, certification requirements, the evolution from Quake-era console commands to modern data-driven systems.

- **"Accessibility and Remapping" (line 396-404):** Also thin. States "Player-accessible remapping is not a luxury feature" but does not back it up with the industry context that makes this claim concrete (certification requirements, legal requirements, accessibility awards).

- **"Multiple Controllers" (line 151-159):** Brief and purely mechanical. Could discuss the history of local multiplayer conventions (arcade cabinets, console split-screen, the renaissance of local co-op on Steam).


### 5. Missing synthesis at section ends

The didactic pyramid calls for each section to close by "connecting back to the broader architecture or contrasting with the alternatives that were ruled out." Several sections just stop:

- **Edge Detection** ends with a benefit statement but does not connect to how edges feed into the action mapping layer or the fixed-tick problem.
- **Analog Inputs** ends with the `ControllerState` struct. No synthesis.
- **Multiple Controllers** ends with a pragmatic note about four slots. No architectural connection.
- **Mouse Delta Accumulation** ends mid-thought about high-polling-rate mice.


### 6. Redundancy between sections

- The **Quarantine Principle** (line 31-47) and **The Polled Snapshot Model** (line 275-289) cover overlapping ground. The polled vs. pushed comparison appears in both. Consider whether the polled snapshot discussion in section 3 can be tightened to refer back rather than re-argue.

- The **timeline of event processing** (line 292-309) largely restates what was covered in the quarantine section and in Chapter 4's INPUT phase description.


### 7. Opening could be stronger

The opening (lines 3-9) is competent but could be more engaging. Compare with Chapter 04's opening, which tells a concrete story about *Dark Souls*' weapon degradation and *Skyrim*'s physics chaos. Chapter 05's opening lists problems abstractly ("let the player rebind keys, swap between keyboard and gamepad"). Consider opening with a concrete shipped-game story about input gone wrong: the infamous *Elden Ring* PC launch with no mouse/keyboard rebinding, or the *Cyberpunk 2077* input latency issues, or the *No Man's Sky* PC port that shipped with no key rebinding.


### 8. Minor issues

- **Line 219:** `key_pressed("jump")` is shown as a migration source, but `key_pressed` takes a `KeyCode`, not a string. This is misleading. Should be `key_pressed(KeyCode::Space)` or similar.

- **Line 453-454:** The borrow-checker explanation about `push_map`/`pop_map` is implementation minutiae. The general principle (separating read operations from write operations on input state) is good, but the Rust-specific borrow-checker detail is too deep for the architectural level of discussion.

- **Platform conventions paragraph (line 393):** The Cross/Circle and Switch A/B topic is fascinating industry material that deserves a callout box, not a single sentence. The history of Japan vs. West confirm-button conventions, Sony's 2020 decision to standardize on Cross globally, and Nintendo's reversed face buttons are all rich stories.


### 9. What is working well

- The **quarantine principle** framing is excellent and well-argued.
- The **Action Mapping Layer** historical grounding (Quake through UE5 Enhanced Input) is the gold standard for what every section should aspire to.
- The **Fixed-Tick Edge Problem** section with its three cases and the Glenn Fiedler citation is thorough and well-structured.
- The **Intent Pattern** code example is clean and the callout warning about automatic buffering is well-reasoned.
- The chapter summary and review questions are strong.
- No em dashes or emojis.
- Word count is within target.
- Opening connects to Chapter 4 and closing tees up Chapter 6.
